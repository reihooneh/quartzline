What it does

Follows the heat into the clay

The pot is cut into rings a few millimetres apart, and each wall into six layers. Heat reaches the faces from the kiln by radiation and convection, reaches the base from a shelf that lags behind, and flows through the clay. The inside of a narrow bottle sees less of the kiln than the outside does.

Turns uneven heat into stress

Hot clay wants to be bigger. Where it's held back by cooler clay nearby, the wall is pulled or squeezed. Quartz and cristobalite make it worse: they change size suddenly at 573 °C and around 226 °C. A Weibull model turns the pull on every bit of wall into a chance of cracking.

Plans the fastest safe firing

It starts with everything flat out. It finds the riskiest moment and slows down whatever caused it: heating through quartz, cooling through it, or opening the lid too soon. Then it speeds everything else back up. You get a schedule your kiln controller can run.

How a dunt happens

  1. 1

    The rim cools first

    Thin and open to the kiln on both faces, the rim loses heat faster than the thick base, which sits on a shelf that is still warm.

  2. 2

    Quartz shrinks at 573 °C

    As the rim passes 573 °C its quartz grains shrink at once. The middle, still hotter, hasn't yet.

  3. 3

    The rim is pulled apart

    The shrinking rim is held at its old size by the hotter clay inside it, so it's stretched round its circumference. If the pull beats the clay's strength, a crack runs in from the edge.

Quartzline is open source (MIT). It runs entirely in your browser: no sign-in, no tracking, and setups you load never leave your device. Its clay and kiln figures are typical values for comparing schedules, not measurements of your clay or your kiln: test a new schedule on pieces you can spare, and follow your kiln maker's safety instructions.
